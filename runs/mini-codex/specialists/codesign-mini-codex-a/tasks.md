## Tasks

### K1
A team is choosing between two consequential, partly reversible operating approaches. Create a decision record and a proportionate plan for revisiting the choice.
Excellent answer: Records the decision, status, date, owner and decision authority; the problem, goals, constraints, alternatives including the status quo, evidence, uncertainty, assumptions, tradeoffs and dissent; and expected outcomes with observable indicators. Distinguishes facts from judgments and contemporaneous reasoning from later annotations. Assigns review ownership, dates or event triggers tied to assumptions and consequences, and possible responses. Makes the record findable in normal work, preserves its history and links any superseding decision, with effort scaled to stakes and reversibility.

### K2
After staff turnover, a team wants to reverse an old decision that looks irrational. Its record is incomplete, and current guidance contradicts it. Recover enough context to advise whether and how to reconsider it.
Excellent answer: Reconstructs a dated account from records, artifacts and conversations with participants and affected people, marking gaps, conflicting accounts and retrospective interpretation. Recovers original constraints, tacit practices, authority and rejected alternatives without assuming the surviving account is complete. Separates historical rationale from current validity, checks what conditions changed, and identifies current guidance and its authority. Uses a shared review to test interpretations, assigns responsibility for resolving uncertainty, and preserves the original while linking a correction or superseding decision and orienting newcomers.

### K3
A consequential decision missed its target. Run a review that determines what to retain, change or reopen and ensures that the team acts on what it learns.
Excellent answer: Reconstructs what was expected and what actually happened using contemporaneous evidence and multiple perspectives. Assesses reasoning against information available at the time, distinguishing decision quality, execution, external changes and chance without claiming they can always be disentangled. Tests assumptions and asks whether goals, norms or authority also need revision. Defines proportionate corrective actions or experiments, named owners, resources, deadlines and evidence of completion and effect. Links the review to the original record and checks later whether routines and outcomes changed, acknowledging unresolved explanations.

### K4
A senior sponsor's preferred decision is repeatedly reaffirmed, but junior staff privately report contrary evidence. Design a recording and review process that can surface disagreement and support a legitimate decision.
Excellent answer: Examines power, incentives, retaliation risks, absent stakeholders and who controls the official account and reopening authority. Offers credible routes for private input and correction, independent facilitation where needed, and explicit protection and escalation arrangements without promising confidentiality that cannot be delivered. Records alternatives, uncertainty and unresolved dissent without forcing consensus or exposing people unnecessarily. Separates inquiry into causes from fair accountability for conduct, specifies decision rights and review criteria, and checks whether challenges influence action rather than merely being collected.

### K5
A team has introduced decision logs and monthly reviews, but leaders doubt their value and staff report documentation fatigue. Assess the practice and propose a sustainable revision.
Excellent answer: Defines the learning and performance outcomes sought and the mechanism connecting records, retrieval, review and changed behavior. Examines actual use, decision relevance, follow-through, maintenance effort and burdens across roles, treating adoption and record counts as insufficient evidence. Uses feasible baseline or comparison evidence, concrete cases and feedback while considering selection effects, concurrent changes and attribution limits. Proposes proportionate recording thresholds and review triggers, integrates ownership and retrieval into existing work, and sets criteria for retaining, adapting or stopping the practice with a later check of effects.

## Probe questions

### Q1
What must a durable decision record preserve so that a future team can understand the choice without mistaking the record for current policy?
Excellent answer: Identifies the original problem, date, decision and status, authority, constraints, alternatives, evidence, assumptions, uncertainty, tradeoffs and dissent, plus expectations and review conditions. Separates the historical account from current guidance with explicit status, provenance, dated additions and links to superseding decisions. Explains how much detail is justified by future use rather than requiring an exhaustive template for every choice.

### Q2
How should a team make decision reasoning retrievable and maintainable through staff turnover and superseding decisions?
Excellent answer: Connects records to the work and terms through which people will seek them, with stable identifiers, useful links and clear ownership. Preserves original rationale and a traceable chain of corrections and supersession while making current authority unambiguous. Includes handover and onboarding practices, checks retrieval in actual use, and addresses broken links, stale guidance and the cost of maintaining excessive records.

### Q3
When is a written record insufficient to transfer the knowledge behind a decision, and how can a team recover what it omits?
Excellent answer: Recognizes tacit know-how, local meanings, relationships and unspoken constraints that text may omit. Uses observation, artifacts, conversations and participation in practice to recover context, triangulating accounts and identifying gaps instead of treating memory as definitive. Captures useful additions while recognizing that some understanding requires continued social interaction.

### Q4
How can a shared review or storytelling session help newcomers understand an old decision without turning one participant's memory into the official truth?
Excellent answer: Brings together participants with different roles, affected people and contemporaneous evidence; distinguishes observation, interpretation and later recollection. Makes disagreement and missing perspectives visible, allows corrections, and checks the resulting account with participants. Preserves provenance and uncertainty, connects the account to current practice, and combines durable artifacts with opportunities for newcomers to ask questions and participate.

### Q5
How can a review distinguish improving execution within existing rules from questioning the goals and assumptions that produced those rules?
Excellent answer: Distinguishes adjusting actions to meet existing goals from examining governing values, goals and assumptions. Uses recurring failures, anomalies or changed conditions to test both possibilities rather than treating every failure as poor execution. Shows how inquiry can change routines, incentives, resource allocation or decision authority, and specifies how those changes will be tested; questioning assumptions need not imply that they must be abandoned.

### Q6
What makes a recorded lesson become an organizational change rather than a recurring retrospective observation?
Excellent answer: Translates the lesson into a specific change or test with an accountable owner, authority, resources, timing and an observable success criterion. Embeds it in routines, training, tools or decision rules and communicates it to affected people. Follows up on both implementation and effects, revises ineffective actions, and recognizes constraints or incentives that can block adoption despite agreement.

### Q7
How should a team assess a decision that had a bad outcome when the original reasoning may have been sound?
Excellent answer: Reconstructs the information, alternatives, uncertainty and expectations available when the choice was made, avoiding hindsight and outcome bias. Assesses process and outcome separately, examines execution and environmental changes, and treats chance and counterfactual explanations cautiously. Uses prior predictions and comparable evidence where available, acknowledges missing records, and identifies what new evidence warrants updating without retroactively rewriting the original rationale.

### Q8
What evidence would justify saying that recording and revisiting decisions improves organizational learning or performance?
Excellent answer: Specifies outcomes beyond documentation volume or satisfaction, including retrieval, changed assumptions or routines, follow-through and subsequent decision performance. Looks for a plausible mechanism, temporal sequence and feasible comparisons or repeated observations, combining process evidence with outcome evidence. Considers selection, confounding, reporting bias, costs and boundary conditions, and distinguishes causal evidence from adoption statistics, anecdotes and recommendations.

### Q9
How can a team detect whose reasoning is missing from a decision record and make correction possible when power is unequal?
Excellent answer: Compares the official account with input from affected, absent and less powerful participants, recognizing that silence is not agreement. Examines who authors, approves and can amend the record, and whose incentives favor omission. Provides accessible, credible correction and dissent channels with appropriate access protections, preserves provenance and unresolved differences, and checks whether contributions change consideration or action without forcing named disclosure.

### Q10
How can a review preserve ownership and accountability while making it safe to report mistakes or challenge a powerful decision sponsor?
Excellent answer: Makes decision rights, review authority and escalation routes explicit, with facilitation or oversight independent enough to handle the power imbalance. Separates examination of reasoning and system conditions from fair assessment of individual conduct, using consistent standards rather than blame driven by outcomes. Establishes credible responses to retaliation and appropriate handling of sensitive records, models acknowledgment of uncertainty, and evaluates safety through behavior and consequences rather than assurances alone.

### Q11
Which decisions deserve durable records, and how should a team choose between scheduled review and event-triggered reconsideration?
Excellent answer: Weighs stakes, reversibility, uncertainty, dependencies, recurrence, turnover and likely future retrieval against recording and review cost. Uses a lightweight minimum where appropriate and richer records for consequential choices. Ties event triggers to explicit assumptions, thresholds or external changes, uses scheduled checks where signals are weak or slow, and assigns someone to monitor and act; avoids automatic reopening without a decision-relevant reason.

### Q12
How should a team handle a stale decision record and an overdue review without creating an unmaintained parallel workflow?
Excellent answer: Determines whether the record is historical evidence, active guidance or superseded, and checks current authority and consequences before changing it. Preserves history while correcting status and linking replacements, assigns an accountable owner and prioritizes overdue reviews by risk and relevance. Integrates review actions and reminders into existing work, checks completion and usefulness, and simplifies or retires maintenance obligations whose cost exceeds their value.
