Preserve decision context, constraints, consequences and superseded rationale in short records that stakeholders can access.
Recover local authority and operating conditions before judging old choices; check actual tool use after formal acceptance.
Treat withheld concerns and fear of questioning goals as barriers to collective learning.
Turn reviews into prioritized, owned, tracked actions with verifiable end states, and examine later incident behavior.
Align debrief purpose, participants and measurement; preserve uncertainty and distinguish case reports and associations from causal evidence.

### F1
Experts: Align debrief participants, purpose, focus and measurement; distinguish evidence for debriefing from evidence for decision logs. Across 46 samples (N = 2,136), debriefs outperformed controls by d = .67, approximately 25% on average; this is not a promised gain for a particular team or logging practice.
Cue: A team needs evidence that its review practice improves performance.
Case: none
Evidence: moderate
Source: S1 — [Do Team and Individual Debriefs Enhance Performance? A Meta-Analysis](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0018720812448394), accessible abstract only.

### F2
Experts: Preserve the forces behind a significant decision, including political and social constraints, and unfavorable consequences. Use short, numbered records; retain reversed decisions with superseded status and a replacement link. Changed context signals reconsideration.
Cue: A newcomer questions an old choice or circumstances change.
Case: Michael Nygard's November 2011 report describes ADR use since August across several projects. Six to ten rotating developers appreciated the recovered context; this supports perceived usefulness, not measured performance improvement.
Evidence: moderate
Source: S2 — [Documenting Architecture Decisions](https://cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions), Decision and Experience Report.

### F3
Experts: Record longer-term intentions before current work obstructs them, and check whether stakeholders can access the repository where reasoning lives.
Cue: Present stabilization work coexists with planned rearchitecture, or readers seldom use version control.
Case: Nygard's 2011 account describes clients stabilizing systems ahead of rearchitecture; recording intentions helped avoid obstructing future changes. His teams shared rendered GitHub links with stakeholders. No comparative outcome or maintenance-cost estimate is reported.
Evidence: moderate
Source: S2 — [Documenting Architecture Decisions](https://cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions), Experience Report.

### F4
Experts: Recover authority, cost and local operating conditions before treating a historical choice as irrational; an apparently obvious alternative may have been unavailable or ineffective.
Cue: Someone asks why technicians repaired equipment instead of replacing it.
Case: Orr's 2006 author note 7 explains that the Xerox machines observed cost $50,000–75,000 in 1984; technicians lacked replacement authority. Location or use could cause faults, so replacement might not help; moving machines could introduce problems and disrupt customers for one or two days.
Evidence: moderate
Source: S3 — [Ten Years of Talking About Machines](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0170840606071933), accessible author note 7.

### F5
Experts: Examine actual incorporation into daily work after formal acceptance; approval does not establish consistent use or close the learning question.
Cue: Managers consider an accepted tool a settled success.
Case: Orr's 2006 author note 2 recounts managerial outrage at his proposal to study an accepted suite of technicians' computer tools. Despite prior testing, use still varied markedly. The note supplies no quantified performance effect.
Evidence: moderate
Source: S3 — [Ten Years of Talking About Machines](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0170840606071933), accessible author note 2.

### F6
Experts: Treat private concerns missing from collective discussion as a learning failure signal; an unchallenged account need not indicate agreement.
Cue: People report problems privately while withholding questions, help or criticism publicly.
Case: Edmondson's 1999 manufacturing-company study describes Publications and Help Desk teams stuck in patterns of withheld concerns. Publications members reported feeling unheard and receiving little honest feedback. The 51-team cross-sectional study supports associations, not causality or a tested confidential-channel remedy.
Evidence: moderate
Source: S4 — [Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Leadership/Edmondson_1999_Psychological_safety.pdf), pp. 374, 377–379.

### F7
Experts: Examine whether fear of management prevents questioning the goal itself, rather than interpreting repeated work as productive experimentation.
Cue: A team perfects a preferred solution before seeking feedback.
Case: In Edmondson's 1999 account, NPD 2 pursued detailed paths it later abandoned; members feared management sanctions for questioning the desired solution. NPD 1 instead reported rapid iteration and trust in colleagues' decisions. These contrasting cases do not isolate a causal intervention.
Evidence: moderate
Source: S4 — [Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Leadership/Edmondson_1999_Psychological_safety.pdf), pp. 374–375, 379.

### F8
Experts: Trace review findings into implemented safeguards and later incident behavior; distinguish practitioner attribution from controlled causal evidence.
Cue: A similar failure recurs after corrective work.
Case: Google's August 11, 2014 satellite disk-erasure incident prompted automation audits and checks. The authors report that implemented actions reduced the spread and rate of a similar incident three years later.
Evidence: moderate
Source: S5 — [Postmortem Culture: Learning from Failure](https://sre.google/workbook/postmortem-culture/), Case Study.

### F9
Experts: Convert lessons into prioritized, owned, tracked actions with verifiable end states.
Cue: Review conclusions lack a delivery mechanism.
Case: Google's actual 2014 satellite postmortem lists owners, priorities and tracking bugs for input rejection, destructive-operation limits and an emergency stop; published identities and capacity values include substitutions.
Evidence: moderate
Source: S5 — [Postmortem Culture: Learning from Failure](https://sre.google/workbook/postmortem-culture/), Good Postmortem and action tables.

### F10
Experts: Preserve uncertainty and source limitations in the official account.
Cue: Monitoring stopped covering affected infrastructure.
Case: Google's actual 2014 satellite postmortem flags unreliable monitoring, explains estimated query losses and leaves revenue impact uncertain. Its deliberately bad teaching example is not a separate historical incident.
Evidence: moderate
Source: S5 — [Postmortem Culture: Learning from Failure](https://sre.google/workbook/postmortem-culture/), Impact.
