# Research plan

Field: Organizational learning: how teams record decisions and revisit them

## Research questions

1. What should teams record about a decision—context, alternatives, assumptions, evidence, dissent, ownership, and expected outcomes—to make it useful to future readers?
2. How do teams integrate decision records into everyday work, keep them discoverable, and preserve their meaning as people and circumstances change?
3. What events or review schedules should trigger revisiting a decision, and how do teams assign responsibility and act on the review?
4. How can teams distinguish a poor decision process from an unfavorable outcome, and test prior assumptions without hindsight bias?
5. How do psychological safety, power, incentives, and organizational memory affect what teams record, whose reasoning survives, and whether decisions can be challenged?
6. How do retrospectives, after-action reviews, decision logs, and other learning practices turn recorded experience into changed routines and future decisions?
7. What evidence shows that recording and revisiting decisions improves learning or performance, under which conditions, and at what maintenance cost?

## Sources that count

- Peer-reviewed empirical papers, systematic reviews, and scholarly books on organizational learning, organizational memory, decision processes, and team reflection.
- Primary research reports and documented organizational cases with a stated method and traceable evidence.
- Practitioner posts, books, talks with accessible transcripts or slides, and postmortems by named practitioners describing actual decision-recording or review practices and their consequences.
- Original decision-record templates or guidance from identifiable organizations, when they explain the practice's rationale, use, or limitations; a template alone supports only claims about its structure.

Anonymous advice, promotional material without substantive evidence, unattributed summaries, search snippets, and AI-generated summaries do not count as evidence. Secondary summaries may help locate an original source but are not substitutes for it.

## Inclusion rules

- A logged source must directly address recording decision reasoning, preserving or retrieving that reasoning, revisiting decisions, or converting decision review into organizational learning.
- It must have an identifiable author or responsible organization, a traceable publication location, and enough accessible content to assess its claims. Record publication dates when available; do not infer missing dates.
- Empirical work must state its method, setting, or evidence base. Practitioner accounts must describe a concrete practice and an actual case, observable result, or explicit limitation.
- Include both academic and practitioner evidence, seeking different organizational settings and evidence about failures, tradeoffs, and boundary conditions as well as successes.
- Separate demonstrated outcomes from proposed mechanisms and recommendations. Do not infer causal improvement from adoption or a favorable anecdote alone.
- Exclude generic knowledge-management advice without a decision or review connection, individual journaling without team implications, duplicate retellings of the same evidence, and inaccessible sources whose relevant claims cannot be checked.
- Opinion-only sources may be logged only when stronger evidence does not cover a relevant question; mark them as weak and explain the gap. Do not treat them as evidence of effectiveness.

## Stop rule

Use at most 5 searches in the search step, run as one round of 5. Count each search query against this budget. Stop when the budget is exhausted, even if questions remain unresolved. The general early-stop rule is to stop after two consecutive rounds of 5 searches add no new finding; that condition cannot be reached within this five-search budget. Report remaining evidence gaps rather than extending the budget. Step 1 performs no searches.

## Viewpoints

### 1. Codified organizational memory
Position: Make decision reasoning durable and retrievable through structured records, explicit ownership, and links between original and superseding decisions.

- What context, alternatives, assumptions, and expected outcomes must a record preserve for a future team to understand the choice?
- How should teams distinguish a historical record from current guidance while preserving the reasoning behind both?
- Who maintains the records, and how will people find relevant decisions during later work or staff turnover?

### 2. Situated and social learning
Position: Records gain meaning through shared practice and conversation; documentation alone may lose the tacit knowledge needed to interpret a decision.

- Which parts of a decision can be recorded, and which require discussion with people who experienced the situation?
- How can newcomers recover the local constraints and informal knowledge behind an apparently puzzling decision?
- When do shared reviews or storytelling help more than a more detailed template, and how can their lessons survive team turnover?

### 3. Reflective learning and questioning governing assumptions
Position: Reviews should examine the goals, norms, and assumptions that shaped a decision as well as whether execution achieved its intended result.

- Is the team correcting an action within existing rules, or questioning whether the rules and goals themselves remain appropriate?
- What would reveal that a once-reasonable assumption has become invalid, and who can initiate reconsideration?
- How does a retrospective produce changes to routines, resource allocation, or decision authority rather than only a written lesson?

### 4. Evidence and decision-process evaluation
Position: Judge decisions using information available at the time and compare expectations with outcomes, while separating learning claims from evidence of actual improvement.

- What predictions, uncertainties, and review criteria should be recorded before outcomes are known?
- How can a review separate sound reasoning followed by bad luck from weak reasoning followed by a favorable result?
- What evidence would show that recording and revisiting decisions improves later choices, rather than merely accompanying an effective team?
- How should a team interpret mixed results when the decision, implementation, and surrounding conditions all changed?

### 5. Psychological safety, power, and contested memory
Position: Decision records reflect whose voices and interests can be expressed; useful review depends on making dissent and challenges possible without retaliation.

- Whose alternatives or objections are missing from the official account, and how can less powerful participants correct it?
- What makes people willing to record uncertainty or mistakes when records may later be used to assign blame?
- How can teams retain accountability and ownership while making reviews safe enough for honest learning?
- Who has authority to reopen a decision, especially when its original sponsor still controls resources or evaluation?

### 6. Practitioner workflow and maintenance economics
Position: Recording and review must fit everyday work, with effort proportional to a decision's stakes, reversibility, and likely future relevance.

- Which decisions warrant a durable record, and when does documentation cost exceed its likely learning value?
- Should a decision be revisited on a schedule, when a recorded assumption fails, or when a specific operational signal appears?
- How can review ownership and follow-through be integrated into existing work without creating an unused parallel process?
- When is it better to retire or supersede a record than to keep updating it, and how will teams detect stale guidance?
