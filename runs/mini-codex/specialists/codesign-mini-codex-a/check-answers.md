# Answers with findings

### K1
Create a short, numbered record with a stable link. Include date, status, owner and decision authority; the operating problem and intended outcomes; options A, B and the status quo; and the evidence, constraints, assumptions and uncertainty behind the choice. Preserve political and social constraints as well as technical or financial ones, and state unfavorable consequences and unresolved dissent. Distinguish observations from judgments and record longer-term intentions that current work could obstruct.

For the partly reversible choice, identify which parts can be changed cheaply and which create durable dependencies. Name the observable outcomes and assumption changes that would warrant reconsideration, an owner to watch them, and a scheduled check for consequences that emerge slowly. At review, the available responses should include continuing, adjusting implementation, experimenting with the alternative, or superseding the choice. This review design is a practical application of the findings, rather than a tested template in them.

Keep the record accessible from normal work, including a readable link for people who do not use the underlying repository. Preserve the original reasoning and date later additions. If reversed, mark it superseded and link the replacement. Scale detail to the consequences and likely future need for context.

Findings used: F2, F3, F10

### K2
Treat apparent irrationality as a question about the old operating conditions before treating it as an error. Recover the decision's date, authority, costs, local constraints, rejected alternatives and intended future direction from surviving artifacts and conversations with participants and affected people. Orr's Xerox example illustrates why this matters: replacement was expensive, outside technicians' authority, and might not fix a fault caused by location or use.

Build a dated account that identifies its sources, conflicting recollections and gaps. Do not silently fill missing rationale with a plausible modern explanation. Ask whether people withheld concerns from the original collective account, especially where private accounts differ. Check who now has authority over the conflicting guidance and which assumptions or circumstances have changed; historical justification and present validity are separate questions.

Review the reconstruction with participants and current decision makers, assign unresolved questions to an owner, and decide whether further investigation, a bounded experiment or a new decision is warranted. Preserve the old record, annotate its uncertainty and status, and link any authoritative replacement. Use the resulting account in handover, with access that newcomers and affected stakeholders can actually use.

Findings used: F2, F3, F4, F6, F10

### K3
Start with the original expectations and reconstruct the actual sequence from contemporaneous artifacts and people in different roles. Mark estimates and missing observations explicitly: Google's satellite postmortem retained uncertainty about losses where monitoring was unreliable. A missed target alone does not establish that the original reasoning was defective. Examine assumptions, execution and changed conditions, while leaving competing explanations unresolved where the evidence cannot separate them.

Ask whether the team was free to challenge the goal and preferred solution, as well as how well it executed. Edmondson's NPD cases show why repeated detailed work can conceal fear of questioning management's desired direction. Invite concerns that were withheld publicly and check whether the account omits them.

For each supported lesson, decide what to retain, change or reopen. Turn changes into prioritized actions or bounded tests with an owner, authority, resources, deadline, tracking location and verifiable end state. Link these to the original decision and review. Check both delivery and later effects in ordinary work; Google's reported reduction in a later similar incident illustrates the value of following safeguards into subsequent behavior, but is practitioner attribution rather than controlled causal proof. Retain uncertainty about why results changed.

### K4
Treat the difference between public reaffirmation and private contrary evidence as a warning that the official account may be incomplete. Examine who sets the agenda, authors and approves the record, can reopen the choice, and bears the consequences of dissent. Check whether staff fear sanctions for challenging the sponsor's goal, rather than assuming they merely need more opportunity to speak.

As a process recommendation, use a facilitator with enough independence to hear contrary evidence, offer private input with explicit limits on access and confidentiality, and establish a credible escalation and anti-retaliation response. Include affected and absent stakeholders. Record the alternatives, evidence, uncertainty and unresolved disagreement without requiring unnecessary attribution to vulnerable individuals. Specify who decides and what evidence would trigger reconsideration; legitimate authority need not depend on manufactured consensus.

Separate inquiry into reasoning and operating conditions from any fair review of conduct. Test the process by whether challenges receive a reasoned response, affect consideration and lead to appropriate action, and by what happens to people who raise them. The findings support the diagnosis of withheld concerns and fear of management; they do not establish that a particular confidential channel or facilitation arrangement will solve it.

Findings used: F2, F6, F7, F9

### K5
First state what the practice is meant to accomplish: recover rationale after turnover, expose changed assumptions, and turn review findings into changes that improve work. Then examine actual cases along that chain: could someone retrieve and understand a relevant record, did a review alter a choice or routine, were actions completed, and did later behavior or outcomes improve? Formal acceptance and record counts do not establish use; Orr reported markedly varying use of tools that managers considered accepted.

Sample recent decisions and reviews, including unused records and overdue actions. Gather feedback across roles about retrieval barriers, time spent writing and reviewing, and whose effort the process consumes. Compare a feasible baseline, repeated periods or similar work, while allowing for selection, other changes and reporting bias. The debrief meta-analysis supports debriefing under studied conditions, not a promised gain from decision logs; Nygard's experience supports perceived usefulness, not measured performance improvement.

Revise toward short records for significant choices, richer context where consequences warrant it, accessible links, named maintenance owners and reviews tied to consequential assumption changes. Fold action tracking into existing work. Set a later check and explicit retain, adapt or stop criteria based on useful retrieval, changed behavior, effects and burden. Preserve historical rationale when retiring a maintenance obligation. These thresholds and evaluation arrangements are recommendations, not experimentally validated rules in these findings.

### Q1
Preserve the dated decision, status and authority, original problem, alternatives, evidence and uncertainty, assumptions, constraints, intentions and consequences, including unfavorable and political or social consequences. Record expectations and what change in context would prompt review. Retain dissent where known rather than treating an unchallenged account as agreement.

Keep original reasoning separate from dated later annotations. Make superseded status and the replacement link explicit so that historical rationale cannot easily be mistaken for current authority. A short numbered record may suffice; add detail according to what future readers will need, rather than treating completeness as unlimited transcription.

Findings used: F2, F3, F6, F10

### Q2
Use short numbered records with stable links from the work they explain. Preserve the original rationale, mark reversals as superseded, and link the replacement so a newcomer can follow both history and current status. Assign someone to maintain that chain and verify the authority of current guidance.

Check access in practice: Nygard shared rendered links because stakeholders might not use version control. Incorporate records into handovers and ask newcomers to retrieve the reasoning for an actual decision. Repair access or stale links revealed by that exercise and remove unnecessary maintenance burden. These handover and maintenance checks extend the findings operationally; the reported ADR experience does not quantify their effects or cost.

Findings used: F2, F3, F5

### Q3
A record is insufficient when it omits local conditions, practical authority, cost, relationships or constraints that made an alternative unavailable. The Xerox repair example shows that an apparently simple replacement option can depend on knowledge of equipment cost, customer disruption and where faults originate. Private concerns may also be absent from a public account.

Recover context through artifacts, conversations with participants and affected people, and observation of current practice where relevant. Compare accounts and identify what remains unknown; memory is another source to evaluate, not a definitive correction. Add useful, dated context to the record, while preserving access to people and practice where a written explanation still leaves questions. The findings illustrate omissions and recovery targets, rather than demonstrating that text can capture all tacit knowledge.

Findings used: F4, F5, F6, F10

### Q4
Organize the session around the original record and contemporaneous artifacts, with participants from different roles and people affected by the choice. Ask what constraints and authority existed then, and distinguish documented observations, later recollections and present interpretations. The Xerox case supplies a useful prompt: which apparently available options were actually beyond the actors' authority or ineffective locally?

Invite missing concerns without treating silence as assent. Preserve conflicting accounts, source attribution where appropriate and gaps; let participants correct the resulting account. Link a dated account to the historical decision and current guidance, and let newcomers ask follow-up questions. These are safeguards inferred from the findings about omitted context, withheld concerns and uncertain evidence, rather than a validated storytelling-session method supplied by the extracts.

Findings used: F2, F4, F6, F10

### Q5
Ask two distinct questions: how could the team execute better against its existing goal, and what evidence challenges the goal, assumptions or preferred solution itself? Recurring abandonment of detailed work or reluctance to seek feedback should prompt the second question. In Edmondson's NPD 2 case, fear of management sanctions inhibited questioning the desired solution; detailed effort therefore did not necessarily indicate useful learning.

Examine incentives and authority that make questioning costly. A review may retain the goal while improving execution, or reopen it and change routines, resources or decision rights. Translate whichever response is justified into an owned test or action with observable results. Questioning a premise does not itself establish that the premise is wrong, and the contrasting NPD cases do not isolate a causal remedy.

Findings used: F5, F7, F9

### Q6
A lesson needs a delivery mechanism: a specific prioritized action or test, an accountable owner, tracking, and a verifiable end state. Google's satellite postmortem used owners, priorities and tracking bugs for safeguards such as destructive-operation limits and an emergency stop. Set authority, resources and timing so the owner can actually deliver it, then embed the change in the relevant tool or routine and inform affected people.

Follow up on both completion and use. Formal approval can coexist with inconsistent daily adoption, and closing a task does not establish effectiveness. Inspect later incidents or comparable work for the intended change in behavior and results; revise ineffective safeguards and investigate practical or incentive barriers. Google's later incident is evidence of reported follow-through and benefit, with causal attribution still limited.

Findings used: F5, F8, F9

### Q7
Reconstruct the information, alternatives, constraints, uncertainty and expectations available at the time. Recover local authority and cost before declaring the choice unreasonable: the Xerox case shows how present-day intuition can overlook constraints on the original actors. Preserve the original rationale and identify new evidence separately.

Assess what the outcome tells you about assumptions and execution without making the bad outcome the sole test of reasoning quality. Compare expected and actual behavior, consider changed conditions and possible chance, and retain competing explanations where records cannot distinguish them. Google's explicit uncertainty about incident impact models restraint when evidence is incomplete, but these extracts do not provide a validated method for separating luck from decision quality. Update the current choice when warranted without retroactively rewriting what was known.

Findings used: F2, F4, F10

### Q8
Look for a chain from accessible rationale to actual retrieval, informed reconsideration, completed actions, changed routines and subsequent outcomes. Combine concrete cases and process evidence with repeated observations or feasible comparisons, and measure the effort imposed. Check temporal order, selection, reporting bias and concurrent changes before attributing performance gains to the practice.

The findings support different claims at different strengths. The 46-sample debrief meta-analysis reports an average performance effect of d = .67 across its studied settings; it does not establish the effect of decision logs or promise a team's gain. Nygard's rotating developers appreciated recovered context, which supports perceived usefulness. Orr's accepted tools still varied in use, warning against adoption as an outcome. Google's reported safeguards and later incident supply follow-through and practitioner-attributed benefit, not a controlled estimate. A credible claim should state which of these levels its own evidence reaches and its costs and boundary conditions.

Findings used: F1, F2, F5, F8, F9

### Q9
Compare the official account with concerns from affected, absent and less powerful people. Private reports alongside public silence are a direct warning sign; Edmondson's Publications and Help Desk cases show withheld concerns, and NPD 2 shows fear of sanctions for questioning a desired solution. Examine who writes, approves, can amend and can reopen the record, and what incentives may suppress evidence.

Provide accessible ways to propose corrections and dissent, including private input with clearly stated handling limits when needed. Avoid forcing named disclosure; retain provenance with access appropriate to sensitivity, distinguish claims from established observations, and preserve unresolved differences. Check whether contributions receive consideration and can change action, rather than counting submissions. These channel designs are recommendations: the findings establish associations and illustrative cases, not a tested confidential-channel remedy.

Findings used: F6, F7, F10

### Q10
Make decision and review authority explicit, including a route outside the sponsor's control when the sponsor is implicated. Use facilitation or oversight sufficiently independent for that route to be credible. State how sensitive reports will be handled and how retaliation will be addressed; promises of safety alone are inadequate when staff fear management sanctions.

Separate investigation of reasoning and system conditions from fair assessment of conduct under consistent standards. Preserve uncertainty rather than manufacturing certainty or judging solely from outcomes. Keep ownership concrete through prioritized, tracked corrective actions with verifiable end states. Evaluate safety by whether people raise concerns, how leaders respond and what consequences follow. The extracts support the importance of withheld concerns, management fear and action ownership; they do not validate a complete accountability or anti-retaliation procedure.

Findings used: F6, F7, F9, F10

### Q11
Start with significant decisions whose rationale future readers will need, particularly choices with unfavorable consequences, social constraints, long-term intentions or likely turnover. Weigh the cost of recording against stakes, uncertainty, dependencies and how difficult reversal will be. Use a short minimum record, expanding where future understanding warrants it.

Changed context is a reason to reconsider. Translate important assumptions into observable event triggers, with an owner to monitor and act; use scheduled checks when changes are slow or signals unreliable. A review can confirm the choice, change execution or supersede it and should have a decision-relevant purpose. These selection criteria and scheduling arrangements operationalize Nygard's significant-decision and changed-context guidance; the findings do not establish optimal thresholds or review frequency.

Findings used: F2, F3, F9, F10

### Q12
First establish whether the stale record is historical rationale, active guidance or already superseded, and who has current authority. Preserve the original account; correct status with a dated annotation and link an authoritative replacement where one exists. Check whether changed context or uncertain evidence makes the overdue review consequential now, rather than reopening automatically because a date passed.

Assign an owner and prioritize by current consequences and relevance. Track review work and resulting actions in the team's existing delivery system, with verifiable completion. Make links usable by affected stakeholders. Check that the result enters daily work, since acceptance and publication alone do not establish use. Simplify or retire low-value maintenance obligations while preserving historical context, then inspect whether the revised process actually reduces burden and supports retrieval. The findings support these components but do not supply a measured maintenance-cost threshold.
