### K1

Create a dated decision record with a stable ID, accountable owner, decision authority, scope, and status. State the problem, intended outcomes, constraints, affected groups, and the two approaches, including doing nothing if that is a credible alternative. Capture the evidence available at the time, uncertainties, assumptions, evaluation criteria, tradeoffs, dissent, and why the selected approach is preferable. Describe which commitments are reversible, the costs and limits of reversal, and any irreversible consequences. Separate the historical rationale from links to current operating policy.

For implementation, assign owners, resources, milestones, outcome measures, and guardrails. Where practical, stage the commitment through a pilot or reversible phase. Record a baseline and what results would count as success, tolerable variation, or grounds to stop.

Set one initial review date aligned with when useful evidence should exist. Add explicit triggers such as a guardrail breach, a failed critical assumption, a material environmental change, or unexpectedly high reversal costs. Give someone responsibility for monitoring and convening the review. At review, choose to retain, adjust execution, expand the trial, or reopen the decision; document the reason and next checkpoint. Match review effort to stakes and uncertainty rather than revisiting the entire choice every month.

### K2

Treat the apparent irrationality as a question to investigate. Preserve the old record and label its incompleteness; establish which current guidance is authoritative, its effective date, and whether it actually supersedes the decision or applies to a different scope.

Build a short timeline from available records and independent accounts: the original problem, alternatives, constraints, evidence, authority, expected benefits, and subsequent changes. Speak with former participants, implementers, affected people, and people who disagreed. Ask what they knew then, what pressures or dependencies mattered, and what would have made a different choice possible. Attribute recollections, distinguish documented facts from interpretations, and leave unresolved conflicts visible. Do not reconstruct certainty where evidence is missing.

Compare recovered assumptions with present conditions. An understandable original choice can still warrant reversal today. Recommend retaining, modifying, or replacing it based on present benefits, harms, obligations, dependencies, and transition costs. If uncertainty is consequential, use a bounded trial or further investigation. Obtain the appropriate authority, write a new linked decision, mark the old record superseded when appropriate, and update current guidance and operating procedures. Keep the historical record intact.

### K3

Run a review with the decision owner, implementers, affected stakeholders, and an appropriately independent facilitator. Start with the original target, assumptions, expected range of results, and information available when the choice was made. Establish an outcome timeline and compare actual results with the baseline, forecasts, and any credible comparison group.

Investigate competing explanations: execution gaps, resource constraints, measurement problems, changed conditions, chance, or a flawed causal model or goal. Examine both confirming and contrary evidence. Separate decision quality from outcome quality and distinguish reasonable uncertainty from ignored warnings. Identify what worked and should be retained as well as what failed.

Classify responses explicitly: improve execution within the existing choice; change an operating rule; or reopen the decision, its assumptions, or its goals. Assign each accepted change an owner, authority, resources, due date, and verification measure. Put actions in the team's existing work system and update the relevant procedure, training, tooling, or policy. Link those changes to the review. Schedule a proportionate effectiveness check; completion means evidence of changed practice or results, not merely a closed action item.

### K4

Separate the sponsor's authority to decide from the process for gathering and evaluating evidence. Publish decision criteria, assumptions, review triggers, and the identity of the decision authority. Collect independent written assessments before group discussion, solicit views from people closest to implementation, and explicitly seek disconfirming evidence.

Use a facilitator outside the sponsor's reporting line where feasible. Offer confidential reporting and explain its practical limits, access rules, and escalation route. Aggregate concerns carefully to reduce identification risk, prohibit retaliation, and provide a route to report it. Do not promise anonymity that a small team cannot deliver.

Maintain a record of material alternatives, contrary evidence, unresolved questions, and the decision authority's response. Attribute dissent only with suitable consent and access controls. Give contributors a way to correct omissions or misrepresentation. During review, have the sponsor speak after other evidence has been heard; assess evidence against agreed criteria rather than seniority. Reaffirmation requires an explicit explanation of why contrary evidence does not change the choice and what future evidence would. If the sponsor controls the disputed process or retaliation occurs, use independent oversight with authority to act. A legitimate decision need not be unanimous, but its authority, reasoning, treatment of dissent, and avenues for reconsideration must be clear.

### K5

Assess the practice against its purpose: better decisions, faster recovery of context, useful reconsideration, and completed improvements. Sample consequential decisions and recent reviews. Test whether newcomers can find the rationale, whether records changed an actual action, whether review triggers were detected, and whether follow-up changes worked. Gather staff and leader feedback, including whose work or concerns the process excludes. Measure time spent as a cost, not record counts as a benefit.

Replace universal logging with a threshold based on consequence, uncertainty, cross-team impact, longevity, and reversal cost. Use a short default record, expanding only where stakes require it. Record reasoning at the point of decision, link evidence and existing work, and avoid copying the same material across systems. Use monthly meetings to triage changed assumptions, exceptions, and overdue actions; reserve full reviews for selected decisions or explicit triggers.

Assign clear stewardship, automate reminders where useful, and close or archive records whose monitoring purpose has ended. Trial the revision for a defined period. Compare retrieval effort, review usefulness, action effectiveness, and documentation burden with the previous practice. Retain only elements that provide credible value, while preserving any required governance records.

### Q1

Preserve the decision's identity, date, scope, authority, owner, original problem and goals, relevant context, alternatives, evidence available then, assumptions, uncertainty, criteria, tradeoffs, material dissent, chosen action, expected consequences, and reconsideration conditions. Include implementation responsibilities and links to supporting sources where useful.

Make lifecycle status and effective dates explicit: proposed, accepted, superseded, retired, or another defined state. Link to the successor decision and authoritative current policy. Preserve the original rationale with dated corrections or additions so future readers can distinguish historical explanation from present instructions.

### Q2

Use a shared, searchable repository with stable identifiers, consistent titles, a small set of metadata, and links from the work and policies people already use. Index by problem, service, topic, and affected area rather than relying on the names of departed staff. Link predecessor and successor decisions in both directions and retain version history.

Assign stewardship to a continuing role with a named current holder and include transfer in handovers. Keep a concise current-state index that points to authoritative guidance and historical reasoning. Check broken links, ownership, and status during existing change and handover processes. Preserve necessary evidence with appropriate access and retention controls; avoid maintaining duplicate summaries that silently diverge.

### Q3

A written record is insufficient when understanding depends on tacit skill, local relationships, contested meanings, informal constraints, embodied practice, or context that participants assumed everyone knew. A terse record can explain the selected option while omitting why other options were practically impossible.

Recover context through interviews with several perspectives, observation, demonstrations, shadowing, paired work, and examination of contemporary artifacts. Use concrete incidents and counterfactual questions to expose hidden assumptions. Label recovered accounts by source and confidence, validate them with participants and evidence, and preserve disagreements. Convert useful findings into examples, training, or updated records, while retaining opportunities for direct practice where prose cannot transfer the skill.

### Q4

Anchor the session in an explicit timeline and contemporary artifacts. Ask participants to describe what they knew and experienced at the time, separating recollection from documented evidence and present interpretation. Gather initial accounts independently where possible so the first confident story does not anchor everyone else.

Include multiple roles, affected people, and dissenting perspectives; use a facilitator to balance participation. Let newcomers ask why apparently obvious alternatives were rejected. Capture converging accounts, contradictions, missing voices, and unanswered questions with their provenance. Circulate the synthesis for correction and seek corroboration for consequential claims. Publish it as a contextual reconstruction, not a replacement for the original record or an authoritative account based on one person's memory.

### Q5

Ask two distinct questions. First: given the existing goals and rules, did execution work, and how could it improve? Second: are those goals, rules, assumptions, and definitions of success still justified? The first supports improvement within the current frame; the second may require changing the frame itself.

Use evidence to determine which level is implicated. A missed step may call for an execution fix; repeated failures despite compliant execution may challenge the underlying theory. Examine incentives, affected groups, excluded alternatives, and unintended effects. Record which level each proposed action addresses and route changes to goals or governing rules to the appropriate decision authority. Do not assume every failure requires reopening the whole strategy, or that better compliance can repair a flawed goal.

### Q6

A lesson becomes organizational change when someone with authority accepts a specific change, assigns an owner and resources, and embeds it in how work is done. Depending on the cause, this might mean changing a procedure, product, incentive, budget, staffing arrangement, training program, or decision rule.

Track the action in the normal work system, define an observable completion condition and expected effect, and verify adoption and effectiveness after implementation. Preserve the link from evidence to lesson to change. If the same observation recurs, investigate why earlier actions failed or lacked authority rather than recording the lesson again as though it were new.

### Q7

Reconstruct the information, alternatives, constraints, probabilities, and tradeoffs available at the time. Assess whether the process used appropriate evidence, sought contrary views, handled uncertainty honestly, and respected the relevant obligations. Compare the outcome with the predicted range and plausible alternatives; a single bad outcome cannot by itself establish poor reasoning.

Investigate execution, environmental changes, and chance alongside flaws in the original model. Use repeated decisions or forecast calibration where available to distinguish systematic error from variation. Retain sound practices, correct avoidable weaknesses, and update beliefs with the new evidence. Sound original reasoning does not remove the need to reconsider a choice whose future prospects have worsened.

### Q8

Credible evidence would connect the practice to observable learning and useful outcomes: faster and more accurate recovery of context, fewer repeated avoidable errors, better calibrated forecasts, timely responses to changed assumptions, and verified changes in behavior or performance. Measure costs, including documentation time and delayed decisions, as well as benefits.

Use baselines and repeated observations, ideally with comparable teams, staggered adoption, or another credible comparison. Account for differences in decision difficulty, staffing, resources, and other simultaneous changes. Trace some cases from record through review to implemented change and measured result, and actively seek failures and counterexamples. Surveys and record counts can indicate adoption or perceived usefulness, but cannot alone establish improved performance. State uncertainty and avoid causal claims stronger than the design supports.

### Q9

Map who made, implemented, experienced, and challenged the decision, then compare that map with the record's contributors and evidence sources. Look for missing frontline roles, minority views, external stakeholders, people who left, and groups who bear costs without decision authority. Participation totals alone do not show whether their reasoning affected the record.

Invite independent contributions through accessible routes, including confidential channels where appropriate. Have a neutral steward track material concerns and their disposition, provide a correction mechanism, and preserve unresolved disagreement. Apply access controls to sensitive accounts, minimize unnecessary attribution, and explain confidentiality limits. Provide independent escalation and protection against retaliation. Review whether contributions changed the analysis or received reasoned responses; token inclusion is insufficient.

### Q10

Name the decision owner, decision authority, implementation responsibilities, and any shared dependencies. Review observable actions, constraints, information, and consequences rather than attacking character. Make reporting mistakes and uncertainty an expected duty, and distinguish good-faith error from deliberate concealment, retaliation, or reckless disregard through a fair process.

Use an independent facilitator when power relationships could distort evidence, gather input before the meeting, and provide confidential reporting and escalation routes with honest limits. Require powerful sponsors to answer contrary evidence and accept scrutiny. Keep learning discussions focused on understanding and improvement; handle any personnel or disciplinary issues through the appropriate separate process. Maintain accountable owners and tracked corrective actions while avoiding punishment simply for surfacing problems.

### Q11

Prioritize decisions with substantial consequences, lasting commitments, expensive reversal, major uncertainty, cross-team dependencies, novel precedents, or likely future controversy. Also record decisions where regulation or governance requires it. Routine, low-impact choices can remain in ordinary work records unless their cumulative effects warrant review.

Use scheduled review when evidence matures over time, risks accumulate gradually, or monitoring needs a regular checkpoint. Use event triggers for identifiable changes such as failed assumptions, threshold breaches, new obligations, or dependency changes. Consequential uncertain decisions often need both: active triggers and a backstop date. Define the owner, evidence, thresholds, and resulting review action; an automatic review is not an automatic reversal. End unnecessary monitoring when the decision is retired or uncertainty has been resolved.

### Q12

Mark the record's last verified date and current status, identify its accountable owner, and check for superseding decisions and authoritative current guidance. Preserve the historical rationale. Add dated corrections and links; do not silently rewrite the past or assume that staleness invalidates the decision.

Triage the overdue review by current stakes, changed conditions, and exposure. Review promptly if material risk or a trigger is present; otherwise explicitly reschedule or retire the review with a reason and owner. Put that action in the team's existing backlog or governance cadence. Update the current policy and decision index through the normal change process, link the historical record, and remove duplicate reminders or trackers. Make stewardship part of ordinary ownership and handover so maintenance does not depend on a separate neglected workflow.
