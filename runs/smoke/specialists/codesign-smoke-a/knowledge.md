# Knowledge: codesign-smoke-a

Field: Organizational learning: how teams record decisions and revisit them

## Summary

1. A required lessons store is not proof that lessons are used; people mostly learn from reviews and talk with colleagues, and mandates to record and consult decay over time.
2. Judge a lessons or decision store by whether it delivers the right lesson at the right moment, not by how many entries it holds; an unpruned store becomes hard to search.
3. A recorded lesson is unlearned until it shows up in a later design or decision; failures often trace to causes already on record.
4. Recording needs trust, funded time, rewards and a local owner inside the unit doing the work; fear of admitting mistakes and unfunded scope suppress it.
5. Short, regular, facilitated debriefs matched to what is measured improve performance by about 25%, and review effort should scale with how hard a decision is to reverse.

## Kept findings

### F1
Experts: Do not take a required lessons database as evidence that lessons are used. They check where people actually get lessons from, which is usually reviews and talk with colleagues, not the official store.
Cue: A rule says managers must consult and add to the lessons system, but nobody can say when they last found something useful in it.
Case: GAO survey of all NASA program and project managers (GAO-02-195, 30 Jan 2002). NASA rules required managers to review and apply lessons, yet managers named system and engineering reviews, project briefings and informal talk with colleagues as their main sources, not the Lessons Learned Information System (LLIS). In the prior two years 43% had not submitted a lesson, against 23% who had; 27% had not heard of LLIS before the survey. Kennedy Space Center staff met after each shuttle launch to discuss what went well and what to do better.
Evidence: strong
Source: S1 — NASA: Better Mechanisms Needed for Sharing Lessons Learned (GAO-02-195) — https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-02-195.pdf

### F2
Experts: Judge a lessons or decision store by whether it delivers the right lesson at the right moment, not by how many entries it holds. They expect an unpruned store to become hard to search.
Cue: Users say it is hard to "weed through" the store, or reviewing it takes days.
Case: GAO-02-195 (2002). LLIS held over 900 lessons; once entered, a lesson stayed indefinitely and was never reviewed for currency or relevance. 58% of managers said the systems did not let them retrieve the right lessons at the right time; 53% found lessons useful less than 25% of the time. One official said reviewing the database took about two weeks. A respondent: "It is difficult to weed through all the irrelevant lessons to get to the few 'jewels' that you need to find."
Evidence: strong
Source: S1 — NASA: Better Mechanisms Needed for Sharing Lessons Learned (GAO-02-195) — https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-02-195.pdf

### F3
Experts: Treat a recorded lesson as unlearned until it shows up in a later design or decision. When something fails, they ask whether the cause was already on record.
Cue: A failure review finds the root cause in an earlier incident's report.
Case: Mars Polar Lander (launched 3 Jan 1999, lost on landing). The 1992 Mars Observer loss had produced the lesson that downlink telemetry is needed during critical events; seven years later the Lander was designed with no telemetry during entry, descent and landing. A Special Review Board found the probable cause was early engine shutdown from spurious leg-deployment signals. A RAND study (Sarsfield, 2000) cited by GAO named failure to apply lessons already learned as a significant source of NASA failures.
Evidence: strong
Source: S1 — NASA: Better Mechanisms Needed for Sharing Lessons Learned (GAO-02-195) — https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-02-195.pdf

### F4
Experts: Expect people to withhold negative lessons when admitting a mistake may hurt their standing, and to see recording as unpaid extra work. They ask for positive lessons as well as failures.
Cue: The store holds few honest accounts of what went wrong, and people describe lessons as "admissions of failure".
Case: GAO-02-195 (2002). Managers named four barriers: no perceived benefit, lack of time, lack of trust, intolerance for mistakes. 39% said applying lessons adds only some or little to mission effectiveness. One manager: people need to be able to "talk about their mistakes without feeling that they are risking their careers." Managers asked for more positive lessons, for example JPL's Deep Space 1, which kept the same management team from start to end of the primary mission.
Evidence: strong
Source: S1 — NASA: Better Mechanisms Needed for Sharing Lessons Learned (GAO-02-195) — https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-02-195.pdf

### F5
Experts: Back recording and reuse with money, time, and performance reviews, rather than relying on a rule or a tool alone.
Cue: The lessons system runs on a small budget and nobody is rewarded or assessed for sharing.
Case: GAO-02-195 (2002) compared NASA with other organizations. LLIS cost $50,000 to $100,000 a year to run. The World Bank put about 3% of its administrative budget into knowledge management, ran about 120 "thematic groups" (communities of practice), and made knowledge sharing part of every employee's performance review. Ford rewarded employees whose submitted lessons were adopted and assessed managers yearly on knowledge sharing. NASA Langley paid scientists when knowledge they captured was reused. GAO reports these as practices, not measured outcomes.
Evidence: moderate
Source: S1 — NASA: Better Mechanisms Needed for Sharing Lessons Learned (GAO-02-195) — https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-02-195.pdf

### F6
Experts: Know that mandates to record and consult lessons decay and get ignored, so they check whether people know the rule and whether it has been watered down.
Cue: A follow-up review years after a fix finds the same low use.
Case: NASA OIG audit IG-12-012 (6 Mar 2012), ten years after GAO-02-195. Of 28 managers of projects launched Jan 2005 to May 2011, 16 (57%) used LLIS, 12 (43%) contributed to it, only 10 knew of the requirement to contribute, and only 6 called it useful. Policies from 2002 and 2005 required lessons "throughout the project lifecycle, for example, at major milestones"; the policy in force since 2007 did not explicitly require LLIS use. The Chief Engineer had assessed the process agency-wide only once since 2005. OIG concluded LLIS was "of diminishing and questionable value".
Evidence: strong
Source: S2 — Review of NASA's Lessons Learned Information System (NASA OIG IG-12-012) — https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ig-12-012.pdf

### F7
Experts: Look for a local, owned lessons process inside the unit doing the work, rather than assuming central funding drives contribution.
Cue: Contribution rates differ widely between units with similar mandates.
Case: NASA OIG IG-12-012 (2012). From 2005 to 2010 only JPL contributed steadily (71 lessons, 10 to 15 a year); Glenn and Johnson averaged about one a year. Over FY2008 to FY2010 JPL received $70,000 and contributed 35 lessons; Glenn received about $470,000 and contributed 5. The 11 JPL managers surveyed worked mainly through JPL's own internal lessons process. Kennedy's 80 lessons in 2010 were a one-off burst, 78 of them from the Space Shuttle Program.
Evidence: strong
Source: S2 — Review of NASA's Lessons Learned Information System (NASA OIG IG-12-012) — https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ig-12-012.pdf

### F8
Experts: Plan lesson capture as funded work before the team breaks up, and accept that teams often pass lessons on through other routes (the next build in a series, reports, talks) rather than the central store.
Cue: Lessons are left for "project close", and the team is reassigned right after delivery.
Case: NASA OIG IG-12-012 (2012), project managers' own words: lessons from one satellite "were applied to the next satellite in the series" and not entered in LLIS; another shared through the PM Challenge and Masters Forum because LLIS "is just not an effective tool". One manager said capturing lessons is "essentially unfunded scope" on a project that is always short of cost and schedule, and "once a mission is launched, it is quickly disbanded".
Evidence: strong
Source: S2 — Review of NASA's Lessons Learned Information System (NASA OIG IG-12-012) — https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ig-12-012.pdf

### F9
Experts: Use short, regular debriefs (after-action reviews) as a reliable way to turn experience into better performance, for teams and individuals alike.
Cue: Asked whether reviewing past work is worth the time.
Case: none
Evidence: moderate
Source: S3 (Tannenbaum and Cerasoli, Human Factors 55(1), 2013: 46 samples, N = 2,136; debriefs improved performance about 25% over controls, d = .67; similar for teams, d = .66, and individuals, d = .71; real settings d = .56, simulated d = .70; average individual session about 18 minutes) — Do Team and Individual Debriefs Enhance Performance? A Meta-Analysis — https://cebma.org/assets/Uploads/Tannenbaum-Cerasoli.pdf

### F10
Experts: Match the debrief to what is measured (a team-focused debrief judged on team results), and use an impartial facilitator. They do not expect video or other media to add much.
Cue: Designing a review: who attends, who runs it, and what result it is meant to move.
Case: none
Evidence: moderate
Source: S3 (team-focused debriefs measured on team performance d = 1.20 versus d = 0.41 on individual performance; facilitated d = .75 versus unfacilitated d = .25, though few unfacilitated studies; more structure trended better, d = .69, .54, .32, but not conclusive; multimedia aids showed no clear gain) — Do Team and Individual Debriefs Enhance Performance? A Meta-Analysis — https://cebma.org/assets/Uploads/Tannenbaum-Cerasoli.pdf

### F12
Experts: Scale the weight of a decision's process, and of its record and review, to how hard it is to reverse. Hard-to-reverse ("one-way door") decisions get slow, careful process; reversible ones are made fast by small groups. They watch for large organizations applying the heavy process to everything.
Cue: Every decision, small or large, goes through the same approvals and write-up.
Case: none (Bezos, Amazon 2015 shareholder letter, states the principle and the failure mode, slower decisions, too much risk aversion, too little experimenting, but names no specific incident)
Evidence: moderate
Source: S5 — Amazon 2015 Letter to Shareholders (Jeff Bezos) — https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/000119312516530910/d168744dex991.htm

### F13
Experts: Expect project-based organizations to learn poorly between projects because project teams are temporary and projects are complex, and design for that rather than treating it as individual neglect.
Cue: Each new project repeats problems an earlier project already hit.
Case: none
Evidence: weak (only the abstract of Williams 2008 could be checked; the headline survey figures attributed to it by citing sources were not verified and are not used here)
Source: S6 — How Do Organizations Learn Lessons From Projects—And Do They? (Terry Williams, IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management 55(2), 2008) — https://hull-repository.worktribe.com/output/417989/how-do-organizations-learn-lessons-from-projects-and-do-they
